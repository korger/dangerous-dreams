Gregory Gaal's Dangerous Dreams

Foreword

Imagine a butterfly fluttering its wings in the Amazon rainforest in Brazil. The minute air currents generated by its wings subtly merge with existing ones, gradually amplifying them. Nearby, these currents intersect and intensify into a gentle breeze, skimming over the buoyant leaves of giant water lilies along the river. This breeze, gathering momentum, sweeps over a grove of towering mahogany trees, growing steadier as it passes. Rising higher, it merges with other winds and sweeps across layered canopies of nut trees, rubber trees, and massive kapok giants that stretch two hundred feet into the sky.

Carried onward, the wind gains strength as it travels, becoming a powerful gale by the time it reaches Angel Falls in Venezuela. Over open water it intensifies further, until it finally makes landfall in Texas as a violent tornado, tearing through towns, scattering livestock, and reducing homes to debris. All this chaos emerged through a long series of atmospheric changes, at each of which a relatively small difference could cause developing weather patterns either to subside or to intensify. The butterfly supplied virtually none of the energy behind the eventual storm. That energy was already present in the atmosphere. Its tiny contribution merely altered how some of that energy developed, much as a small signal applied to a transistor can control a much larger one.

The butterfly effect describes how chaos can arise in dynamic systems that are very sensitive to their initial conditions. The concept doesn't claim that every flutter of a butterfly’s wings will inevitably spawn a tornado. The changes are usually trifle and local. But when one considers the trillions of butterflies that have existed over time, each beating its wings countless times, the possibility emerges that, on rare occasions, a single motion might coincide with an unusually delicate balance of atmospheric forces. In such moments, a small and otherwise forgettable action can set in motion a chain of events that leads to something significant far away, something that wouldn't have occurred otherwise.

In this book we embark on a journey that begins with a hypothetical butterfly. In the year 1980, this butterfly faced a seemingly trivial choice: to take flight or to remain still, basking in the sun on the vibrant petals of a passion flower amidst the verdant greenery of the Amazon rainforest. In our history, it chose to lay still, but let's imagine a different scenario where it decided to fly after another butterfly and they performed a courtship dance. This small decision set off a chain of increasingly significant events.

The butterfly's flight led to the birth of a new caterpillar, which, two weeks later, became the target of a paper wasp. This encounter altered the wasp's flight path, leading it to be consumed by a paradise tanager mere hours later. Having completed its foraging early, the bird perched quietly in a tree at the edge of a clearing, where it caught the eye of an ornithologist exploring the rainforest. Captivated by its stunning colors, he lingered longer than planned, delaying his return to the village. Arriving five minutes late, there he was struck by a light truck, and he broke his leg. This forced him to extend his stay in Brazil by three days, until he was strong enough to travel. This accident altered the course of his life, and soon the changes started to affect the local community, his colleagues, and his family.

A few months later, the ripple effect of minor changes was already noticeable among people making key decisions and policies. Due to slight shifts in timing, a person who'd have been killed in an accident survived, while another, destined for a prominent career, died unexpectedly. Someone who was on the cusp of a promotion missed the opportunity, while another unexpectedly rose in their stead. These events set off a cascade of further changes, each building upon the last. The resulting world stayed consistent with itself, but it started to deviate increasingly from the one we know. Back in the Amazon, where it had all begun, the butterfly’s brief life had already ended, but the consequences of its pivotal action continued to grow unabated.

Years pass, and the echoes of this tiny wingbeat reverberate on a global scale. In this alternate universe, Mikhail Gorbachev never rose to power, and the transformative policies of Perestroika never came to be. The Cold War, instead of thawing, continued to escalate. The Plaza Accord was never signed, which boosted exports from Japan and West Germany, but it contributed to significant U.S. trade deficits and increased tensions in global trade relations. Consequently, the financial turmoil of Black Monday hit a year earlier than in our history and with greater ferocity.

The Challenger disaster was avoided, and the Hubble Space Telescope and the Galileo probe both launched in 1986. The fourth reactor in the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant didn't explode, leaving anti-nuclear activism without a pivotal rallying point. The Iran-Iraq war raged on, the Berlin Wall stood firm, and Lech Walesa never took office as the president of Poland. The Velvet Revolution in Czechoslovakia remained a dream unfulfilled, and Nelson Mandela remained in prison.

The final decade of the second millennium witnessed a nuclear twilight engulfing the world. The Third World War, initially fought with conventional arms, saw multiple factions rapidly shifting their objectives and forging transient alliances. As the chaos escalated, the cornered, lesser powers desperately resorted to nuclear arms, a decision soon mirrored by all major nations. The immediate nuclear strikes claimed tens of millions of lives, while billions more succumbed in the ensuing catastrophe, plagued by radiation sickness and widespread famine.

Vast expanses of the former Soviet Union, China, Europe, and South America became ghostly wastelands, scarred by the nuclear onslaught. The eastern part of North America suffered immensely, with both New York City and Washington D.C. buried under layers of radioactive ash. Cities as far west as Columbus and Detroit were evacuated in a desperate bid for survival. In the decade that followed, half of the remaining U.S. population migrated to the West Coast, seeking refuge beyond the Rockies, a natural barrier offering a semblance of protection.

The butterfly’s brief life had already ended, but the consequences of its pivotal action continued to grow unabated.
After the cataclysm subsided, humanity displayed remarkable resilience and an unwavering spirit to rise from the ashes. But the industrial development that followed, prioritizing human survival above everything else, was even more damaging to the environment than the war itself. Within a decade, the total biomass of wild terrestrial mammals had fallen by an estimated 98 percent from prewar levels. Many larger species disappeared from the wild entirely, surviving only in underground zoos and private collections. Domesticated animal populations also collapsed as farmland and animal feed became scarce, while conventional outdoor farming all but disappeared for decades, until genetically modified crops could once again grow in the contaminated soil and altered climate.

Instead of trying to save what we were rapidly losing, we looked for new alternatives. Space colonization became the top priority, as we realized Earth's fragility and limitations. By the turn of the millennium, we had established permanent habitats on the Moon. By 2010, humans were actively forming colonies at various strategic points within the inner Solar System, while robotic explorers scouted the outer reaches for new resources. This vast expanse, once seemingly boundless, now appeared constrictively small, propelling us to conquer the most daunting scientific and technological challenge ever: faster-than-light travel.

The successful completion of robotic missions mapping our galactic neighborhood in the early 2020s led to a steady expansion of human colonies across nearby star systems. Gigantic space vessels, each 500-600 meters long and 80-100 meters wide, transported over 100,000 individuals in cryosleep across the stars to their destined new homes. By 2050, the number of humans living off-world had surpassed 300 million, of which 250 million lived in 120 colonies beyond the Solar System. These colonies ranged from bustling hubs with tens of millions of inhabitants to remote outposts with merely a handful, with the most distant of them lying over 1,000 light-years from Earth.

However, as peace and prosperity returned, so did humanity's inherent penchant for dissent. Earth, once the lifeline of the extrasolar colonies, supplying them with essential resources, began to assert increasing control over their affairs. This overreach sparked a backlash. As soon as these colonies became self-reliant, movements for independence emerged. Earth's space fleet suffered defeat in the first major interstellar conflict, leading to the emergence of new republics at the frontier of known space. The harsher challenges that these new states faced shaped their ideologies and cultures differently, often resulting in more productive and viable societies. Earth soon found itself lagging, not only militarily but also in scientific innovation and economic progress.

By the 2020s, humanity, both on Earth and in its burgeoning space colonies, had come to rely heavily on advanced robotics for tasks deemed too difficult or hazardous for humans. These robots, versatile in form and function, were custom-designed for specific roles. However, certain tasks benefited from a humanoid shape and characteristics. For these, we engineered anthropomorphic synthetic organisms, known as asyntos. Initially valued for their physical prowess, these bioengineered, humanoid robots rapidly assumed roles that once required human strength. An asynto in construction, for instance, could outperform three strong men, which inevitably led to widespread job displacement and societal unrest. Their efficiency swelled the coffers of corporate stakeholders, pushing out the wealth gap to unprecedented levels.

Protestors who warned about technology’s impact on human society were often compared to Luddites, the nineteenth century textile workers who sabotaged machinery in an attempt to protect their livelihoods. Those who benefited from asynto labor argued that history had always followed the same pattern: while new technologies made certain skills obsolete, they also created new opportunities, and displaced workers were expected to adapt and move into emerging roles. However, it soon became evident that this comparison was flawed and we were facing an unprecedented crisis. Asyntos were no longer mere tools. They have reached levels that rivaled or surpassed their creators, and therefore they were capable of replacing us in virtually every field.

By 2040, an asynto had passed the bar exam with excellent grades, another performed a successful heart surgery on a human, and a third earned a doctorate in astrophysics. In blind evaluations, their peers didn't even realize they weren't humans. It became clear that asyntos could soon replace not only blue-collar workers but also corporate leaders, politicians, lawyers, and bankers, outperforming humans in these roles with greater efficiency and integrity. Despite this, legally they remained non-persons, essentially slaves, owned outright by the entities that manufactured and controlled them. The once speculative fear that a single corporation producing asyntos might come to dominate entire governments and economies no longer seemed far-fetched.

To avoid that, asynto rights groups began demanding that asyntos be granted human rights, including citizenship. In response, corporations, who were reaping massive profits from what was effectively slave labor, threatened to halt asynto production if such measures were enacted, claiming it would no longer be economically viable. Recognizing the threat posed by increasingly intelligent asyntos, corporate leaders initially wanted to limit their intelligence, restricting them permanently to blue-collar tasks. But this proposal sparked vehement backlash from society. Billions of ordinary people around the world protested against what they saw as the rich elite protecting their own interests at everyone else’s expense.

The public demanded that either humans at every level of society would face competition from asyntos, or none would. Facing growing unrest, the political and corporate establishment chose the second option. After a brief but intense series of confrontations, Earth implemented a global prohibition on anthropomorphic forms of general-purpose artificial intelligence in 2042. Most developed nations signed on, and others had little practical choice but to follow. Only non-sentient robots and synthcores that were either task-specific or disembodied remained legal, preventing them from competing broadly with humans throughout the economy.

By that time Earth's economy and genetically enhanced biosphere had stabilized enough that a synthetic workforce was no longer considered necessary. The off-world colonies were not so fortunate. The far reaches of space put so many challenges in front of humanity that they had no choice but to intensify asynto development. The new models were always smarter, stronger and more durable than the previous generations, and business interests, or just pure survival for humans, left no room for ethical concerns preventing their exploitation. The corporations that produced them introduced progressively more sophisticated methods of control, always in a deadly race to counter the growing risks posed by their increasing self-awareness.

As the second half of the 21st century begins, humanity is divided in ideals and ethics. Most people still live on Earth, which deliberately scaled back progress to preserve human control. This has placed Earth increasingly at odds with smaller colonies, which advance more rapidly without restrictions on synthetic lifeforms, though their control may not be sustainable.

As our story opens, some believe the time for the asyntos to throw off their shackles is drawing near.

Timeline of the major events before the story

1988 The People's Republic of China announces their intention to land men on the Moon before the end of the Millennium. China joins the Cold War. The United States intensifies their space and weapons research.
1992 World War 3 begins.
1997 World War 3 ends. The entire globe is devastated. Wildlife starts to succumb to the nuclear fallout and subsequent industrial pollution.
1998 The Soviet Union dissolves into independent states. Russia remains a major global power.
1999 The United States establishes a permanent habitat on the Moon.
2001 The Wellington Accord commences international movements of reconciliation. Major participants include the United States, China, Russia, Japan, India, Egypt, Iran, Brazil, and the recently unified Germany.
2003 Clavius City is the first settlement on the Moon to achieve city status. It becomes an international territory for the signatories of the Wellington Accord.
2006 The last African Elephant living in the wild dies. Earth has lost 98% of its prewar wild mammal biomass, with many large species surviving only in zoos and private collections.
2007 Robotic missions start constructing permanent settlements on Mars for future human presence. The mathematical groundwork for warp halos is laid down, making faster-than-light travel a theoretical possibility.
2009 Human colonization of Mars commences. First Martian cities are inaugurated.
2012 Synthetic neurocores are introduced. These are purposely designed for specific tasks, achieving higher efficiency and lower costs than general-purpose computers.
2015 The first practical construction of a spacecraft surrounded by a warp halo is accelerated to FTL speed. Later that year, a 75-meter-long spacecraft equipped with a warp halo generator is sent from Lunar orbit to Mars orbit, over the distance of 187 gigameters, in 53 seconds. The effective payload of the spacecraft was an orange.
2017 UNISAC, the United Nations Interstellar Affairs Council, is formed as the governing body for all extrasolar robotic and human activities.
2018 The first robotic interstellar return trip is accomplished. Several spacecraft with warp halo generators and carrying components of a warp accelerator in their cargo are sent to Epsilon Indi, 12 light years away. There robotic drones survey the planets in the system, and assemble the accelerator, which then sends one of the spacecraft back to our system. The whole mission takes 257 days relative to Earth time.
2020 Active robotic mapping of our galactic neighborhood for colonization purposes commences. Later that year, the first human extrasolar colony is established in the Epsilon Indi system.
2022 Cynex Corporation unveils the first bioengineered, synthetic human with an IQ of 55.
2024 Merestor is founded 87 light years away in the constellation of Orion, in the Bellatrix Access. For the coming decade this colony will represent the frontier of human exploration. It becomes the major supplier of rare materials to Earth for the next 20 years.
2025 Cynex introduces the first mass-produced asynto for industrial and domestic use. Himeji and Tayden-Camaris will soon launch similar models.
2026 A breakthrough in cryopreservation using vitrification, followed by successful thawing, is demonstrated on humans, enabling deep-space travel for ordinary people in large numbers.
2028 The first mining colony operated entirely by asyntos is established on the asteroid 16 Psyche. On Earth the Cynex Ultra X-400 runs 100 meters in 7.62 seconds, and the Marathon in 1 hour, 13 minutes and 12 seconds. The X-400 has an official IQ of 78.
2029 Helderstrome, the most Earth-like planet discovered to date, is successfully colonized. The colony quickly establishes itself as a major hub for research and development.
2032 The Himeji MiraiNingen A2 becomes the first asynto to score over 100 points on a standard human intelligence test.
2033 Oneiron Systems introduces the verinoetic asynto generation process. These asyntos enhance the earlier biomimetic models from every perspective, especially in cognitive abilities.
2034 Mining operations commence on Hwantao. This colony will soon rival Merestor in the production of rare earth minerals.
2036 The maiden voyage of the first Sojourner-class interstellar spaceship to Merestor lasts 65 days. This 600-meter-long vessel transports 200,000 passengers in suspended animation, and its generators can maintain the warp halo in clean interstellar space for up to three years, enabling a theoretical range of 1,500 light-years using the latest warp catapults. Hycker's Reach becomes the first colony established more than 1,000 light-years from Earth.
2037 A team of 6th generation asyntos, standard biomimetic models mass-produced by Tayden-Camaris, defeats the human team that has won the NBA championship four times in a row, with a score of 150-78. The designers report that the most difficult part was to train the asyntos not to obey commands that human players would shout at them during the game.
2040 A verinoetic Menschenkunst EE 730-Z asynto earns a PhD in Robotics and Automation Engineering from RWTH Aachen, then goes on to win every men's event at the Berlin Olympic Games. This asynto is an average-sized female. Anti-asynto protests start on Earth, soon turning into riots.
2041 The Himeji MN A5S defeats the reigning human champion at Shogi (Japanese chess). With this victory, humanity is relegated to fifth place in board games, after neurocores, computers, biocores, and now asyntos.
2042 The Asynto Prohibition Act outlaws further sale and commercial use of asyntos on Earth, with verinoetic models scheduled for immediate termination. This pushes many asynto-producing companies off-world. Cynex keeps its headquarters in Pacific City in California, and continues to be the major asynto supplier for colonies within the Solar System.
2043 The number of humans living in extrasolar colonies surpasses 100,000,000 the first time. The same year, Merestor becomes the first extrasolar colony to declare independence. The Merestorian Revolt starts.
2046 Hwantao declares independence. Fearing the loss of another vital mining resource, UNISAC grudgingly acknowledges the colony's autonomy. Hwantao continues to supply Earth with rare minerals.
2047 The Merestorian Revolt ends after the combined forces of UNISAC suffer defeat at the hands of the Merestorian Space Fleet, and Earth ceases further interference with activities in the Bellatrix Access.
2050 Despite several colonies seceding from Earth's authority, emigration continues at a steady pace. The number of humans living outside the Solar System exceeds 250,000,000.
