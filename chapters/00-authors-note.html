Gregory Gaal's Dangerous Dreams

Author's Note

My objective in writing this novel was twofold. First, I wanted to explore what it means to be human, and whether our lives have a deeper purpose. Second, I wanted to examine how societies are shaped by the environments in which they develop, and what happens when cultures that have evolved under radically different conditions finally meet.

Many accomplished authors have dealt with these questions before, both in serious and entertaining ways. I wanted to offer my own perspective, exploring these themes through an adventure story without giving up any of the depth. Along the way you'll read about detectives, artificial humans, extrasolar colonies, forgotten communities, and people struggling to find their place in a world transforming faster than they can follow. Through their journeys, I hope to explore humanity and purpose from a personal angle.

To understand my choice for the setting of my story, it helps to begin with the world we already know. A familiar, contemporary environment might have been easier to follow for readers, but I found it too restrictive for the story I wanted to tell. For example, modern transportation and communication have connected much of humanity into a single global culture. Today, you can travel anywhere in the world in less than a day, and you see the same airports, same shopping malls and five-star hotels everywhere. The financial districts of big cities all look the same. Young people dress alike, listen to the same music, and follow the same trends and memes online. While rapid communication and transportation have brought many benefits, they leave less room for those distinct societies that interested me. I wanted to imagine societies shaped by drastically different environments, separated by distances so great that they develop their own culture and moral standards, which others consider unfair or even dangerous. Then I wanted to see what happens when all these people finally interact with each other as they are in pursuit of more resources and territory.

Isolated societies were common in the past, and they may become common in the future again. For my goals, the future offered more opportunities, since it also allowed me to explore what it means to be human in a technologically advanced world where the line between humans and machines gets increasingly blurry. In the cyberpunk genre, people augment themselves with technology to become stronger, more durable, smarter, or just better connected to society. They replace parts of themselves with machines. In contrast, the possibility of creating human-like artificial organisms with superior skills and qualities pushes the boundary between humans and machines from the opposite direction. Both ideas have been chewed to the bone in previous works, but rarely together. I thought that if we ever developed the technology to pursue one direction, we would probably have the technology to pursue the other as well. So I wanted to explore what happens when both occur at the same time. If humans become more machine-like while machines become more human-like, what happens to humanity?

To play around with these ideas I needed a degree of freedom that only a fictitious world can provide. However, when the invented world becomes too detached from our own, any conclusions drawn from it become equally detached. Fiction and fantasy give authors many opportunities to break rules and just invent plot devices to move the story along, whenever that feels convenient. But when anything becomes possible, nothing is challenging. So to keep my world relatable to our own, I wanted it at least to operate according to consistent rules. Technologies used in one setting should be present throughout the whole story, and limitations must constrain everyone equally.

For that reason, among other things, I have tried to maintain scientific plausibility as a consistent framework for my imagined world. I made only one exception. In any future we can presently foresee, communication will remain much faster than transportation. As a result, different societies remain connected even when separated by vast distances, but our definition of "vast" will be confined to the boundaries of the Solar System. In contrast, for my story I wanted to create isolated societies. To achieve that, I introduced interstellar travel and the colonization of distant star systems. As our science stands today, this will never happen in a human time scale, but world building was important so I had to make this concession.

Faster-than-light travel and interstellar exploration are so commonplace in contemporary popular culture that readers will not even bother to consider the paradoxes they open, such as effects preceding their causes or the relativity of simultaneity. I tried to find a solution that allowed me to build my world, which was also relatable to our own, without spiraling into the realm of fantasy. As a compromise, for now I just silently assume that a yet undiscovered physical phenomenon changes the interstellar world back to something more familiar to our own. This is of course very unlikely, but easier to understand for most readers. With that concession in place, I hope I can create a story which is at least logical, even if not entirely scientifically sound.

In spite of my attempt at maintaining consistency with the world as we know it, I want to emphasize that I'm not trying to predict our future in any way. In fact, I wanted to capture a society that evolves under different constraints than ours, which leads to accelerated development, at least from the technological perspective. To make that explicit, the book is set in an alternate history. This history is the same as we know until about the year 1980, at which point it diverges and follows a different path. The world I'm describing is not going to happen in our timeline, because our most recent history has already gone another way. But we share the same ancestry, and everything I'm referring to before 1980 is a historical fact. Everything after it is invention; a future that didn't happen, though it could have.

But if philosophical and scientific questions are not what brought you here, don't worry. At its heart, this is still an adventure story, with a healthy dose of mystery sprinkled in for seasoning. I have planned it as a story spanning many volumes, and this book is only its beginning, introducing the world, its inhabitants, and some of the conflicts that will shape what follows. Most of the questions I have raised here will only be explored in depth much later.

I cannot promise that I will ever reach the end of such a long journey. For now, I'm glad to have started it, and I hope you will read it. Perhaps enjoy it, as well.

One last note. Everywhere in the galaxy, humans use the metric system, except in the United States of America, which continues to use Imperial units. I was bold enough to hypothesize interstellar travel and artificial humans, but the United States ever moving to metric seemed a leap too far.

Waterloo, Ontario, Canada, September 2026.
